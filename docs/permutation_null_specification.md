# Permutation null specification: joint permutation and subgroup-inclusion sensitivity

B = 10,000 replicates, seed 42, every cohort. Generated by `python -m recompute.run_null_joint && python -m recompute.aggregate_null_joint && python -m recompute.report_null_joint`.

## Verdict

**The headline conclusion does not survive under the corrected null -- but not for the reason that seemed most likely going in.**

The manuscript's central claim is that *no clinical cohort produces an Inclusivity gap distinguishable from chance*, with ACS-Income (a non-clinical cross-domain demo) as the sole exceedance. Three separate corrections were applied. They do not behave the same way, and it is important to say which one does the damage.

**1. The joint permutation, on its own, changes essentially nothing.** At the published `m_min = 30`, replacing the independent per-column permutation with a single joint row permutation moves every p-value by at most 0.0135 -- one to three Monte-Carlo standard errors. The set of cohorts exceeding their null at p<0.05 is unchanged (ACS-Income before, ACS-Income after), and ACS-Income remains the only individual exceedance. The diagnosis was correct in direction -- the independent null is misspecified and is the wider of the two in most cells -- but the magnitude is a few thousandths. The reason is that the premise does not hold empirically: pairwise Cramer's V between these demographic columns is about 0.08-0.10, a weak association, not a strong one (Section 1). The fix is still correct and should be adopted; it simply does not rescue or overturn anything by itself.

**2. The combined clinical test crosses 0.05 -- and it already did at the published settings.** Over the five estimable clinical cohorts, Stouffer's z under the joint null at `m_min = 30` gives p = 0.0485; Fisher gives p = 0.0762. Under the events-based inclusion rule both are decisive: Stouffer p = 0.0007, Fisher p = 0.0016. The manuscript never ran a combined test; it read nine individually non-significant p-values as an absence of signal. Jointly they are not consistent with the null.

**3. `m_min = 30` is doing the work, and it is not a defensible choice.** Under the events-based rule (a subgroup needs >=10 observations in EACH outcome class before its AUROC is treated as estimable) the clinical cohorts NHIS 2024 and NHIS 2023 cross p<0.05 under the joint null (NHIS 2024 p=0.0245, NHIS 2023 p=0.0179). **ACS-Income is no longer the only exceedance.**

The clearest case is NHIS 2023. Its race partition admits levels of n=31 with 5 events, n=34 with 2 events and n=66 with 5 events. An AUROC estimated on two events is nearly pure noise, and noise widens a max-min range. Dropping those three levels barely moves the observed statistic (0.1290 -> 0.1280) but collapses the null's 95th percentile from 0.352 to 0.112. The p-value goes from 0.742 to 0.018. The gap was always there; `m_min = 30` was hiding it behind noise the same rule had admitted.

**4. The cross-cohort sign argument in the manuscript is backwards.** Section 'Cohorts whose measured gap is below its own null mean' reports two of nine and offers it as reassurance. But these nulls are right-skewed (median skew 1.01), so under H0 the mean sits above the median and about 57% of draws fall BELOW their own mean. Under H0 you would expect 5.1 of 9 cohorts below their null mean, not 2. Observed: 7 of 9 cohorts sit ABOVE their null mean against 3.9 expected (binomial p = 0.040). The observation the paper offers as evidence FOR the null is, correctly calibrated, evidence against it.

### What honestly stands

- The strict claim **"no clinical cohort produces a gap distinguishable from chance"** is **not supported**. It holds only under `m_min = 30`, an inclusion rule that was never varied and that admits AUROCs estimated on two events.
- **ACS-Income is not the only exceedance.** Under the events-based rule, NHIS 2024 and NHIS 2023 also exceed.
- The **combined clinical test crosses 0.05** under both nulls and under every inclusion rule by Stouffer; by Fisher it crosses under `m_min >= 50` and the events rule.
- Honest qualification: under family-wise (Holm) correction across the nine tests, only ACS-Income survives at any setting. Under BH at the events rule the survivor set is ACS-Income; NHIS 2023 additionally survives BH at `m_min = 100` (q = 0.048). The individual clinical results are a real crack in the negative conclusion, not a confirmed positive; the combined result is stronger than that.
- The **minimum detectable effect** is the deeper problem. At `m_min = 30` the design cannot detect a subgroup AUROC gap below 0.23-0.37 in four of the five clinical cohorts. A negative result from a procedure with that MDE is not evidence of absence, and the manuscript should not read it as such regardless of what the inclusion rule does.

Pairwise bias-corrected Cramer's V between demographic columns sits around 0.08-0.10 in the clinical cohorts and near zero in the cross-domain ones. That is a weak association, not a strong one. Weak association means the per-column gaps are only weakly positively dependent, so the maximum under the independent scheme is only slightly inflated relative to the joint scheme -- which is exactly what the tables show.

Note that the more intuitive explanation -- 'one partition dominates, so the maximum is that partition and dependence cannot matter' -- is **not** what is happening. In NHIS 2023, German Credit, the synthetic cohort and Adult Income the runner-up partition is within 20% of the leader. Dominance would have predicted a large effect in ACS-Income (runner-up/leader 0.18) and a small one in NHIS 2023 (0.99); the observed p-value shifts show no such pattern.

**The correction should still be adopted.** The independent scheme is a genuine misspecification of the null: it generates demographic assignments whose joint contingency structure does not exist in the data. That it happens to be nearly harmless in these ten cohorts is a property of these cohorts, not of the method, and a cohort with strongly collinear demographics would be materially misjudged by it.

## 2. Subgroup-inclusion sweep

`m_min = 30` had never been varied anywhere in the package. It is the most consequential unexamined choice in the procedure, because the null's width is set by its noisiest admissible subgroups: at NHIS 2024's prevalence of 0.075 a level with n=30 carries about two events, and an AUROC on two events is close to pure noise. Noise inflates a max-min range, which inflates the null, which inflates the p-value.

All five rules are evaluated on the **same** permutation draws, so the settings are exactly paired.

### Reading the sweep

- **`m20`, `m30` and `m50` are almost indistinguishable.** Very few levels sit in 20-50, so the published `m_min = 30` is not a knife-edge in the size dimension. This is worth stating plainly: the problem is not that 30 is the wrong number, it is that *size is the wrong axis*.
- **`ev10` is the principled rule and it is the one that moves things.** What makes an AUROC unstable is events, not rows. NHIS 2024's age partition contains levels of n=107 with 3 events, n=286 with 2 events and n=306 with 5 events. All three clear `m_min = 100`. None of them supports an AUROC. `m100` admits them and drops a BRFSS race level with n=59 and 12 events, which does. `m100` is outcome-blind and therefore incoherent -- it is included in the sweep for completeness, not as a recommendation.
- **The rule filters the point estimate as well as the null**, which is why the observed gap moves too. Two patterns appear. For NHIS 2023 the observed gap is essentially unchanged (0.1290 -> 0.1280) while the null collapses -- the measured gap was real and the noise was hiding it. For NHIS 2024 both move (0.3279 -> 0.1809 observed, 0.3656 -> 0.1636 at p95) -- part of the headline gap WAS the noisy levels, but the null shrank faster, so the residual gap is now significant. Both routes end in the same place.
- **BRFSS 2024 goes the other way under `m100`** (p 0.069 -> 0.467), because that rule amputates the well-estimated small race levels that carry its signal. Under the events rule it stays where it was (p = 0.058). This is the sharpest single illustration that `m100` is the wrong axis.

**The combined test crosses 0.05 under every setting by Stouffer, and under `m_min >= 50` and the events rule by Fisher.** At the published `m_min = 30` the two methods disagree (Stouffer 0.0485, Fisher 0.0762) -- Stouffer weights each cohort's z equally and is the more sensible choice here because the five cohorts are of comparable evidential weight and the alternative is a consistent one-sided shift rather than one extreme cohort. ## 4. Null skewness and the sign of the observed gaps

Every null is right-skewed (skew ~0.9-1.3), so its mean sits above its median and a **majority** of draws fall below their own mean under H0. `docs/cohort_evaluation_results.md` reports that two of nine cohorts measure a gap *below* their own null mean. Under H0 the expected number is about five. Two cohorts is a deficit of five-ish in the direction of the alternative, not reassurance that the gaps are small.

## 5. Monte-Carlo error, the p-value floor, and multiplicity

- **B raised from 2,000 to 10,000** for all ten cohorts. At B=2,000 the MC SE near p=0.08 was about 0.006, the same size as the gap between the two borderline cohorts; at B=10,000 it is about 0.0027. Every p-value in every table carries its MC SE.
- **The floor is reported as an inequality.** With the standard +1 correction the smallest attainable p is 1/(B+1) = 1.00e-04. ACS-Income attains it and is now printed as `<= 1.00e-04`, not as `0.000`.
- **Multiplicity** is controlled across the 9 estimable tests within each (scheme, rule) cell. Holm controls FWER, Benjamini-Hochberg controls FDR. Under the joint null at m_min=30 the Holm survivors are ACS-Income and the BH survivors are ACS-Income. Under the events rule the BH survivors are ACS-Income, with NHIS 2023 and NHIS 2024 next at q = 0.074.
- **Minimum detectable effect** is the null's 95th percentile, tabled as `MDE (p95)` above. It is large: at m_min=30 four of the five estimable clinical cohorts cannot detect a subgroup AUROC gap below 0.23-0.37. That is a statement about the power of the procedure, and it is the strongest reason not to read the negative result as evidence of absence. The events rule roughly halves the MDE for NHIS 2024 (0.366 -> 0.164) and cuts it by two thirds for NHIS 2023 (0.352 -> 0.112).
